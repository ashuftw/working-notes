#### a) Support Vectors and Margin

In the provided figure, the **support vectors** are the data points that lie exactly on the dashed margin lines. Specifically, they are the **two circles (class +1)** and the **one cross (class -1)** that touch the dashed lines.

A **support vector** is a training data point that is critical for defining the decision boundary. These are the points that constrain the position of the hyperplane. The SVM's goal is to find a decision hyperplane that maximizes the **margin**, which is the distance between the hyperplane and the nearest data point from either class. The support vectors are precisely these nearest points that "support" the margins.

---

#### b) Optimization Problem for Separable Data

After crossing out the 2 misclassified data points, the remaining data is perfectly linearly separable. The optimization problem for a hard-margin (zero-error) SVM is to find the hyperplane that maximizes the margin. This is formulated as a constrained optimization problem:

**Minimize:**

$$
\frac{1}{2} \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{w}
$$


**Subject to the constraints:**

$$
y_i(\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x}_i + b) \geq 1 \quad \text{for all } i
$$


Where:
* $\mathbf{w}$ and $b$ define the decision hyperplane.
* $\mathbf{x}_i$ is a training data point.
* $y_i$ is the class label for that point (+1 or -1).

---

#### c) The Role of Support Vectors

The role of support vectors is to **define the optimal separating hyperplane**. They are the only points in the training set that influence the final position and orientation of the decision boundary and the margins.

They are crucial because the solution to the SVM optimization problem (the weight vector $\mathbf{w}$) can be expressed as a linear combination of *only* the support vectors. All other data points, which lie correctly classified and outside the margin, could be removed or moved around without changing the solution. The support vectors contain all the essential information from the training set needed to make a classification.

---

#### d) Multi-Class SVM Methods

Since the standard SVM is a binary classifier, two common methods to extend it to a multi-class problem are:

1.  **One-vs-Rest (OvR):** For a problem with K classes, you train K separate SVM classifiers. The k-th classifier is trained to distinguish data from class `k` (positive class) against all data from the other K-1 classes (negative class). A new data point is classified by assigning it to the class whose classifier reports the highest confidence score.

2.  **One-vs-One (OvO):** For a problem with K classes, you train a separate binary SVM for every possible pair of classes, resulting in $\frac{K(K-1)}{2}$ classifiers. To classify a new data point, it is passed through all classifiers, and the class that "wins" the most pairwise matchups is chosen (majority vote).
