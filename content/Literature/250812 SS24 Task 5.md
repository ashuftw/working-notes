#### a) Overfitting and Performance Monitoring

The phenomenon shown in the graph, where the training loss continuously decreases while the test loss begins to increase after a certain point, is called **overfitting**. The model is memorizing the training data instead of learning to generalize to new, unseen data.

To observe the performance during the long training process without using the final test set, you should split your initial 3000 labeled images into two new sets:
1.  A **training set** (e.g., 2000 images).
2.  A **validation set** (e.g., 1000 images).

You then train the network on the new training set and evaluate the loss on the validation set after each epoch. This allows you to monitor for overfitting as it happens.

#### b) Ideal Curves and Early Stopping

Ideally, by using a validation set, the validation loss curve would track the training loss curve as they both decrease. When the network begins to overfit, the validation loss would bottom out and start to rise, even as the training loss continues to fall.

To prevent the overfitting shown in the graph, you should use a technique called **early stopping**. You would monitor the loss on the validation set and stop the training process at the epoch where the validation loss is at its minimum (around epoch 40 in the provided graph). You would then use the model weights from that specific epoch as your final model.

*(The ideal validation curve would look similar to the green "test data" curve, and you would stop training at its lowest point).*

#### c) The Vanishing Gradient Problem

If the network is not learning at all, a likely cause is the **vanishing gradient problem**. This occurs when the error signal (gradient) shrinks exponentially as it is propagated backward through the network's layers, becoming so small in the early layers that their weights are barely updated.

Looking at the backpropagation formulas, the term responsible for this is the derivative of the activation function, **$f'(v)$**, which appears in both the output and hidden layer sensitivity calculations:

* $\Delta_{i}^{(2)} = \underline{f'(v_{i}^{(2)})} \cdot (z_{i} - y_{i}^{(2)})$
* $\Delta_{j}^{(1)} = \underline{f'(v_{j}^{(1)})} \sum_{i=1}^{N} a_{i,j}^{(2,1)} \Delta_{i}^{(2)}$

For saturating activation functions like `tanh()` or sigmoid, the derivative $f'(v)$ is close to zero for large positive or negative inputs $v$. When these small derivatives are multiplied together across many layers, the overall gradient can "vanish."

---

#### d) Solutions for Deep Networks

For a very deep (e.g., 50-layer) network using `tanh()` that suffers from the vanishing gradient problem, two effective solutions are:

1.  **Change the Activation Function:** Replace the `tanh()` activation function in the hidden layers with a non-saturating function like the **Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU)**. The derivative of ReLU is 1 for all positive inputs, which helps the gradient to flow backward through the network without shrinking.

2.  **Change the Network Architecture:** Introduce **skip connections** or **shortcut connections** that bypass one or more layers. This is the core concept behind **Residual Networks (ResNets)**. These connections create a direct path for the gradient to flow to earlier layers, effectively combating the vanishing gradient problem in very deep architectures.
