#### b) Choosing the Number of Clusters (N)

To choose the optimal number of clusters, one can analyze the distances at which clusters are merged. A good choice for N is often found right before the largest "jump" in merge distance, as this indicates that two relatively dissimilar clusters are being merged.

We can sketch the merge distance `d` for each remaining number of clusters `N`.

| N | 3 | 2 | 1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| d | 3.61 | 4.47 | 5.00 |

The increase in distance `d` when going from N=3 to N=2 is `4.47 - 3.61 = 0.86`. This is the largest increase in the later stages of clustering. This suggests that the most natural grouping of the data is into **N=3 clusters**.

The sketch would fshow the merge distance `d` on the y-axis and the number of clusters `N` (from 9 down to 1) on the x-axis. The point on the curve with the steepest vertical rise indicates the optimal point to "cut" the dendrogram. In this case, the largest jump occurs before merging into 2 clusters, making N=3 the best choice.

#### c) How to Get a Different Dendrogram

The final dendrogram depends on two main choices in the algorithm. Changing either of these will likely result in a different clustering hierarchy.

1.  **The Cluster Distance Definition (Linkage Criterion):** The current approach uses the *minimum* of sample distances (single linkage). We could use a different criterion.
    * **Example:** Use the **maximum** of sample distances between two clusters. This is known as **complete linkage**.

2.  **The Sample Distance Metric:** The current approach uses the **Euclidean distance** ($L_2$ norm) to measure the distance between two individual data points. We could use a different distance metric.
    * **Example:** Use the **Manhattan distance** (also called city block or $L_1$ norm), which is calculated as the sum of the absolute differences of the coordinates.

### Socratic Approach to Hierarchical Clustering

**Q: What is our main goal in this exercise?**

**A:** Our goal is to solve Question 4 by performing hierarchical clustering on a set of 9 data points. We will build a dendrogram, determine the best number of clusters, and explore how changing the rules of the algorithm would affect the outcome.

### Part a: Performing the Clustering

**Q: The problem asks for "hierarchical clustering" using an "agglomerative" approach. What does that mean?**

**A:** It means we will start by treating each of the 9 data points as its own individual cluster. Then, step-by-step, we will merge the two closest clusters until only one large cluster containing all points remains.

**Q: How do we determine which clusters are "closest"? The problem specifies two things: a distance metric and a linkage criterion.**

**A:** 1.  For the distance between two individual points, we must use the **Euclidean distance**: $d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$.
2.  For the distance between two clusters, we must use the **minimum of sample distances**, also known as **single linkage**. This means the distance between two clusters is simply the distance between the two closest points contained within them.

**Q: Excellent. So, what is the very first step in the process?**

**A:** The first step is to calculate all pairwise Euclidean distances and find the smallest one. After calculating all distances, the smallest is between points **x⁽⁴⁾(5, 5)** and **x⁽⁵⁾(6, 6)**, with a distance of $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$.

**Q: What do we do with this information?**

**A:** We perform our first merge. We combine {4} and {5} into a new cluster {4, 5}. We now have 8 clusters, and we record that this merge happened at a distance of **d=1.41**.

**Q: What happens next?**

**A:** We repeat the process. We find the new minimum distance between any two of our 8 current clusters. The next smallest distance is between **x⁽¹⁾(0, 1)** and **x⁽²⁾(2, 1)**, with a distance of **2.00**. We merge them into cluster {1, 2}.

**Q: Let's summarize the entire merge sequence. Can you list each stage, showing the number of clusters (N) and the distance (d) of the merge?**

**A:** Yes. Following the single-linkage rule, the complete process is as follows:

| Stage | # Clusters (N) | Merged Clusters | Distance (d) | New Cluster |
| :---: | :------------: | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| 1 | 8 | {4}, {5} | 1.41 | {4, 5} |
| 2 | 7 | {1}, {2} | 2.00 | {1, 2} |
| 3 | 6 | {7}, {8} | 2.24 | {7, 8} |
| 4 | 5 | {3}, {4, 5} | 2.83 | {3, 4, 5} |
| 5 | 4 | {6}, {3, 4, 5} | 3.16 | {3, 4, 5, 6} |
| 6 | 3 | {7, 8}, {3, 4, 5, 6} | 3.61 | {3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8} |
| 7 | 2 | {9}, {3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8} | 4.47 | {3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9} |
| 8 | 1 | {1, 2}, {3..9} | 5.00 | {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9} |

**Q: How do we visualize this sequence of merges?**

**A:** We use a **dendrogram**. It's a tree diagram that illustrates the merge hierarchy, with the height of each merge corresponding to the distance at which it occurred.

### Part b: Choosing the Number of Clusters

**Q: Looking at the merge distances, they keep increasing. How can this help us choose a "natural" number of clusters?**

**A:** We should look for the largest "jump" in distance from one merge to the next. A large jump implies that we are merging two clusters that are naturally far apart, and perhaps shouldn't be merged. The ideal number of clusters is right before this jump.

**Q: Where is the largest jump in our merge distances?**

**A:** Let's look at the increase in distance at each of the final steps:
* Going from N=4 to N=3 occurred at d=3.61.
* Going from N=3 to N=2 occurred at d=4.47 (an increase of **0.86**).
* Going from N=2 to N=1 occurred at d=5.00 (an increase of 0.53).

The largest jump (0.86) is when we reduce the number of clusters from 3 to 2.

**Q: So, what is the logical conclusion for the best number of clusters, and how would a sketch show this?**

**A:** The logical conclusion is that the data has a natural structure of **N=3 clusters**. A sketch would plot the merge distance `d` on the y-axis against the number of clusters `N` on the x-axis. This plot would show a steep rise between N=3 and N=2, indicating that this is the best place to "cut" the dendrogram.

### Part c: Getting a Different Result

**Q: The dendrogram we created is a direct result of two key choices in the algorithm. If we wanted a different outcome, what is the first choice we could change?**

**A:** We could change the **linkage criterion**. We used single linkage (minimum distance). Using a different rule to measure the distance between clusters would change the merge sequence.
* **Example of a different choice:** **Complete linkage**, where the distance between two clusters is defined by the distance between their *farthest* two points.

**Q: What is the second fundamental choice we made that could be altered?**

**A:** We could change the **distance metric**. We used Euclidean distance ($L_2$ norm) to measure the separation between two individual points.
* **Example of a different choice:** **Manhattan distance** ($L_1$ norm), which measures distance by summing the absolute differences along each axis, as if traveling on a city grid.
